Finland. On New Year’s Eve, Finns practice wax divination, and their festive tables are laden with a wide array of food and drink. A particular favorite among the northerners is a sweet rice porridge served with plum kissel (a fruit-based dessert). The Finnish version of Santa Claus is known as Joulupukki—a name that translates to "Christmas Goat"—so called because he travels in a sleigh pulled by goats. Since the old man has excellent hearing, it is best to whisper your wishes to him, ensuring that no evil spirits overhear.
Sweden. On New Year’s Eve, Swedish children hold an election for Lucia, the "Queen of Light." The girl chosen for this role wears a white gown and a crown adorned with lit candles. Lucia offers treats to household pets and distributes gifts to the children. This festival of light is marked by the glow of bright lights throughout the streets and in homes.
Hungary. As the New Year arrives, Hungarians bring out horns, whistles, and pipes, blowing them as loudly as they can. This ritual is intended to cleanse the home of evil spirits, making room for prosperity and joy. Traditional Hungarian New Year’s dishes often feature peas, beans, nuts, apples, honey, and garlic.
France. In the lead-up to December 31st, the French decorate their homes with wooden and clay figurines. The French Santa Claus, known as Père Noël, leaves gifts for children inside their shoes. The "King" of New Year’s Eve is the child who finds a bean baked inside a festive cake.
Spain. As the local clock chimes, every Spaniard eats a dozen grapes—a ritual believed to bring good luck for the entire year ahead. In fact, Spanish shops even sell jars of seedless, peeled grapes for this purpose. Another interesting custom is that one should ring in the New Year wearing red underwear to ensure financial stability over the next 12 months. This tradition applies to both men and women.
Italy. It is well known that, in keeping with medieval traditions, Italians get rid of old belongings on New Year’s Eve by tossing them straight out of their windows. This custom is not practiced in all major cities; it remains most prevalent in rural areas. On January 1st, Italians often go out to fetch running water to bring home, hoping to encounter a hunched-over old man along the way—a sight believed to bring good luck (unlike running into children or monks). Instead of a "Father Frost" figure, gifts are delivered by Befana, a fairy who flies on a broomstick; however, Italy also has its own version of a magical gift-giving grandfather, known as Babbo Natale. During the celebrations, Italians eat lentils, nuts, and grapes—symbols of health, prosperity, and a long life.
South Africa. Much like in Italy, people in South Africa get rid of old items by throwing them out of their windows. In recent years, however, authorities have sought to ban this practice for safety reasons.
Nepal. In Nepal, people do not simply discard old items; instead, they burn them in New Year’s bonfires. At daybreak, the Nepalese decorate themselves with intricate patterns, dance, and sing to mark this vibrant festival of colors.
England. The English celebrate the New Year in a peaceful yet diverse manner, featuring performances of scenes from fairy tales, carnivals, and street fairs. Instead of hanging a stocking for gifts—as is the custom in many other countries—children place a plate on the table. Shortly before midnight, the silence is broken by the ringing of bells, signaling the arrival of the New Year. At the same time, lovers share kisses beneath a sprig of mistletoe—a romantic ritual believed to protect a couple from breaking up over the next 12 months. A traditional English holiday feast includes turkey served with potatoes, chestnuts, and gravy, alongside meat pies and braised Brussels sprouts. For dessert, there is fruit and pudding.
Germany. The German equivalent of Santa Claus—much like his English counterpart—leaves gifts for children on their plates, though he travels by donkey. Adults pass the time with activities like lead-pouring (divination), board games, and fireworks. Germans generally avoid preparing heavy, elaborate meals for the winter holiday, opting instead for lighter fare such as raclette or fondue. Fish dishes and doughnuts are also popular choices.
Scotland. The Scottish New Year, known as Hogmanay, is celebrated as a festival of fire. Participants burn barrels filled with tar, which symbolize the old year. This practice also pays homage to ancient traditions, in which fire was believed to ward off evil forces; furthermore, casting fire into water was thought to secure the favor of water spirits. In Scotland, the most welcome "first foot"—the first person to cross the threshold of a home on January 1st—is a dark-haired man, who should arrive bearing gifts. According to local belief, a man with dark hair brings good luck. Guests are expected to bring a piece of coal, which is then tossed into the fireplace to ensure good fortune. On New Year’s Day, the Scottish table is set with oatcakes, cheese, and pudding in the morning, followed by steak, goose, apples baked in pastry, or a pie later in the day.
Japan. In the Land of the Rising Sun, the New Year is welcomed in new clothing, a custom believed to ward off illness and invite good fortune. A small tree known as a *motibana* serves as a New Year’s tree, while doorways are decorated with pine branches. Bamboo shoots and branches, as well as miniature peach and plum trees, are also popular decorations. On New Year’s Eve, the Japanese hope to hear 108 bell tolls, a ritual intended to help one become a better person and cast away negativity. Foods such as rice, noodles, beans, and carp are served to symbolize health and family well-being.
Vietnam. For the Vietnamese, the New Year falls sometime between January 21st and February 19th. It is customary to decorate rakes, which symbolize the ability to "rake in" wealth and happiness. The local version of Santa Claus—known as Tao Quen—is believed to be capable of flying through the sky astride a carp and transforming into a dragon. To have a wish granted by this magical spirit, one should whisper it to a real carp and then release the fish into a body of water; the wondrous creature will surely convey the message to the Divine. During the New Year, it is also customary to exchange well-wishes written in black ink on red paper, decorate homes with flowering peach branches, and set off homemade firecrackers. Traditional Vietnamese New Year dishes are primarily made from rice.
Ecuador, Peru. New Year’s celebrations in major cities often involve rituals where people consult with shamans to catch a glimpse of the future. Divination using eggs and beer remains a popular practice, and various superstitions are also widespread. To attract good fortune, Peruvians and Ecuadorians may adorn a beautiful young woman with fruits. Wearing bright yellow is thought to bring happiness, while red is associated with love. Those who dream of spending the coming year traveling should run around their house several times—suitcase in hand—as the clock strikes midnight. To cast off the negativity of the past year, it is customary to toss a glass of water out a window; the act symbolizes the shattering and removal of all the evils present in one’s life.
Colombia. A character known as "Old Year" (El Año Viejo) takes center stage during the carnival. He moves about on stilts, entertaining children with amusing tales. The local equivalent of Santa Claus, called Papa Pascuale, is in charge of the fireworks. Just before the New Year arrives, a parade of effigies—or "dolls"—is held.
Brazil. New Year’s celebrations here are rooted in the observance of the day of Iemanjá, the goddess of the waters and patroness of sailors. People offer her gifts—such as candles and flowers—by setting them afloat on the water, while making wishes. In Brazil, there is no traditional striking of a clock at midnight; instead, the final seconds leading up to midnight are counted aloud. As for gifts, people mostly exchange small souvenirs, since the main presents are given at Christmas. On New Year’s Eve, people dress in white (or in blue, in smaller towns), embrace one another, offer forgiveness, and gather to watch the light show at Copacabana and the fireworks at Lagoa Rodrigo de Freitas. The Carnival season begins shortly after the New Year.
USA. Some of the liveliest and most boisterous New Year’s celebrations take place on Ocean Drive, the main thoroughfare in Miami Beach. Far from being discouraged, unconventional dance styles and elaborate costumes are actively welcomed here. Musicians and performers take to the stage near a Christmas tree adorned with ornaments and garlands. Alternatively, one might celebrate at a café or restaurant featuring performances by stars—often of international renown. Affluent travelers may opt for a New Year’s yacht cruise, watching the fireworks from the deck of a luxurious vessel.
Australia. In the land of the kangaroo, the New Year is celebrated in the summer rather than the winter. The festivities are grandest in the major cities, where a wide variety of performers and artistic groups put on open-air shows. One of the world’s largest fireworks displays lights up the sky over Sydney Harbour. A spectacular vantage point for taking in the pyrotechnics is the Sydney Tower; tickets for this experience are sold in advance. In Tasmania and Victoria, the Falls Festival takes place—an event particularly popular among young people. Across all Australian states, people ring in the New Year with great noise and boisterous celebrations. On January 1st, many head to the beach to enjoy surfing, dancing, and picnics. The festivities continue for another five days.
Panama. During the holiday, the people of Panama strive to be as noisy as possible, believing this will enlist the support of the forces of good.
Burma. The Burmese celebrate the New Year in mid-spring, with the holiday spanning three days. To appease the gods, they hold tug-of-war competitions.
China. During the New Year, people in China perform the ritual of "bathing the Buddha" in temples and engage in the custom of splashing water on one another while exchanging well-wishes.
Israel. In Israel, the New Year arrives in September. Leading up to the holiday, people eat pomegranates, honey, apples, and fish, and offer prayers. Particularly significant prayers to God are recited near bodies of water.
